# Decisions: Why People Choose, Delay, or Avoid Choosing

## Why people DO make a decision
- **A clear, attractive option stands out** relative to the others (salience, contrast).
- **Low friction**: fewer steps, less effort, less uncertainty.
- **Defaults**: the pre-selected option is chosen far more often (organ donation, retirement savings). Defaults are among the most reliable choice-architecture tools (Jachimowicz et al. 2019 meta-analysis; strong-moderate).
- **Social proof**: what similar others do, especially under uncertainty.
- **Deadlines and scarcity**: force a choice, but genuine scarcity only; fake urgency is a dark pattern.
- **Trust**: credible source, guarantees, reversibility ("free returns", "cancel anytime") lower perceived risk.
- **Emotional pull and identity fit**: "people like me choose this".
- **Anticipated regret of NOT acting** (fear of missing out).

## Why people DON'T decide (decision avoidance)
- **Status quo bias and inertia**: staying put requires no justification; switching does. Gal & Rucker argue much of what is called loss aversion is really a preference for inaction.
- **Choice overload**: too many options can lead to no choice and less satisfaction, but the effect is inconsistent. A meta-analysis (Chernev et al. 2015) found it appears when choices are complex, preferences unclear, options hard to compare, or the person must justify the choice. Fix: curate, categorize, give a recommendation.
- **Anticipated regret**: people avoid choices they might blame themselves for (Anderson 2003).
- **Uncertainty and ambiguity aversion**: unknown probabilities feel worse than known risks.
- **Conflict between attractive options**: when no option dominates, people defer (Tversky & Shafir).
- **Effort and cognitive load**: hard comparisons, jargon, too many forms.
- **Fear of judgment**, unclear next step, or feeling it's "not the right time" (present bias).

## Key biases and how solid they are
- **Loss aversion**: the claim that losses weigh about twice as much as gains. A 2024 meta-analysis of 607 estimates found an average of roughly 2 (Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer, J. Econ. Lit.), but other meta-analyses and studies find it much smaller, absent for small stakes, and dependent on context (Walasek et al.; Gal & Rucker 2018). Treat as real but context-dependent, stronger for larger stakes.
- **Endowment effect**: people value what they own more than identical things they don't (moderate; cause debated).
- **Anchoring**: initial numbers pull estimates (robust in many paradigms; weaker incidental anchors).
- **Framing**: "90% survival" vs "10% mortality" changes choices (robust).
- **Present bias / hyperbolic discounting**: immediate rewards loom large; "later" feels cheap (robust).
- **Sunk cost**: continuing because of past investment (present but smaller than folklore in some studies).
- **Decoy effect**: adding an inferior option boosts a similar superior one (mixed replication; works in some settings).
- **Confirmation bias, overconfidence, availability**: robust.
- **Decision fatigue**: the famous parole-judges study has strong alternative explanations; treat as weak.

## Nudges overall
A 2022 meta-analysis (Mertens et al., PNAS) reported medium effects, but reanalyses correcting for publication bias found little or no average effect (Maier et al. 2022; Szaszi et al. 2022), while effects vary a lot by type. Defaults and reducing friction tend to work best; reminders and information alone are weaker. Real-world nudge-unit trials show small but real effects (DellaVigna & Linos 2022: about 1.4 percentage points). Expect small effects.

## Practical decision levers (ethical versions)
1. Reduce options to 3-5 meaningful ones and highlight a recommended pick.
2. Make the next step obvious and small.
3. Lower risk: free trials, easy undo, guarantees.
4. Show honest social proof.
5. Make benefits concrete and immediate; frame consistently.
6. Use real deadlines; never fake scarcity.
7. For important choices, help people slow down (pros/cons, sleep on it), which reduces regret.
