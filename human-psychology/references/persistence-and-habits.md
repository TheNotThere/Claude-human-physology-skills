# Persistence and Habits: What Makes People Keep Doing a Task

## Habit formation
- A habit is a cue -> behavior link learned through repetition in a stable context (Wood & Neal). The reward helps the link form, but once formed the cue triggers the behavior automatically.
- **How long?** Lally et al. 2010 found a median of about 66 days to reach automaticity (modeled range 18-254 days). The first systematic review (Singh et al. 2024, 20 studies, 2,601 adults) found medians of 59-66 days, means of 106-154 days, and individual estimates from 4 to 335 days; only four studies measured timing directly and many had high risk of bias. Stronger habits were linked to frequent repetition, stable context, morning timing, self-chosen behaviors, and enjoying the behavior. The "21 days" claim traces to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 self-help book, not research.
- Missing one day does not ruin habit formation (same study).
- Best ingredients: a consistent cue (time, place, preceding action), a small and easy behavior, immediate satisfaction, and repetition.
- **Habit stacking**: attach the new behavior to an existing routine ("after I pour coffee, I...").
- Breaking habits: change the context (moving, new device layout), add friction to the cue, or substitute a different behavior for the same cue. Willpower alone is a weak strategy.

## Reinforcement schedules (operant conditioning; strong)
- **Fixed ratio** (reward every N actions): high rate, pause after each reward.
- **Variable ratio** (reward after unpredictable number of actions): the highest, steadiest response rates and most resistance to quitting. Slot machines, loot drops, and pull-to-refresh feeds work this way.
- **Fixed interval** (reward after set time): bursts near the deadline. Daily login rewards.
- **Variable interval**: steady moderate rates. Checking email/messages.
- **Near misses** increase continued play in gambling, even though they are losses.
- Unpredictable rewards are powerful because of dopamine prediction error. Use them for delight; avoid pairing them with money in ways that mimic gambling.

## Progress and completion
- **Goal-gradient**: effort speeds up near the finish line. Progress bars and "3 of 5 complete" increase completion.
- **Endowed progress**: giving people a head start increases completion (Nunes & Dreze 2006).
- **Unfinished tasks**: a 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier) found no overall memory advantage for interrupted tasks (Zeigarnik effect), but a reliable tendency to resume them (Ovsiankina effect; roughly two-thirds resumption vs a 50% baseline). Say "people tend to return to unfinished tasks", not "people remember unfinished tasks better".
- **Small wins** (Amabile & Kramer): daily progress in meaningful work is the strongest predictor of good days at work.

## Streaks and loss
- Streaks motivate by building identity and by making a break feel like losing something. Effective but can become anxiety-driven. Streak freezes or forgiveness reduce harm and quitting after a break ("what-the-hell effect").
- After a broken streak, many people quit entirely; design a "fresh start" (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014: new weeks, birthdays, and new years increase goal pursuit).

## Feedback
- Immediate, specific feedback sustains effort. Delayed or vague feedback weakens learning.
- Feedback on effort and strategy supports persistence after failure more than feedback on talent.

## Why people quit
1. Too hard without a path to improve (competence frustration).
2. Too easy or repetitive (boredom; no new challenge or novelty).
3. Feeling forced or manipulated (autonomy frustration).
4. Isolation, or a toxic social environment.
5. Rewards dry up and no internal motivation was built.
6. Life context changes (time, money, new habits).
7. A single big failure or broken streak with no recovery path.

## Grit and mindset — caution
- Grit adds little prediction beyond conscientiousness (Crede et al. 2017 meta-analysis).
- Growth-mindset interventions show small average effects, larger for struggling students in supportive settings (Yeager et al. 2019). Do not oversell either.
