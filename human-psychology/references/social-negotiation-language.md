# Social Cognition, Negotiation, Language, and Resistance

## Social cognition
- **Mentalizing / theory of mind** (medial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction): understanding others' beliefs and intentions takes effort and drops under time pressure or overload. Overloaded people accept claims more readily, so important decisions deserve calm conditions.
- **Empathy** has an affective side (feeling with someone) and a cognitive side (understanding their view). Cognitive empathy helps negotiation; affective empathy drives helping but can cause burnout.
- **Warmth and competence** (Fiske's stereotype content model): people judge others on both. Warmth is judged first and weighs heavily. Competence without warmth reads as cold; warmth without competence reads as nice but ignorable.
- **Status signals**: relaxed, open posture, calm voice, and steady pacing are linked to perceived confidence. Power posing's hormonal effects did not replicate; small effects on felt confidence are debated.
- **Mirroring** posture and language modestly increases liking when subtle and natural; obvious mimicry backfires.
- **Shared identity** (same team, place, or interests) quickly increases trust and cooperation (strong; social identity theory, Tajfel). Must be genuine.
- **Reciprocity**: personal, unexpected, non-monetary favors produce the strongest return.

## Negotiation (practitioner and research knowledge)
Preparation:
- Know your **BATNA** (best alternative if no deal), your walk-away point, and your target. Estimate theirs.
- Separate positions (what they ask for) from interests (why they want it). Fisher & Ury's principled negotiation; strong practical support.
- Rank issues; find low-cost-to-you, high-value-to-them trades.
Opening:
- First offers anchor the outcome (Galinsky & Mussweiler; strong). Anchor first when you have good information; be cautious when you don't.
- Ambitious but justifiable anchors work; absurd ones damage trust.
During:
- **Tactical empathy** (Chris Voss, a practitioner, not researcher): labeling ("It sounds like timing is the real concern"), mirroring key words, and open "how/what" questions help uncover interests and calm tension. Practical value; little controlled research.
- Concede in shrinking steps, ask for something in return, and explain the cost of each concession.
- Name pressure tactics calmly (fake deadlines, "my boss won't allow it", good-cop/bad-cop); naming reduces their effect.
Closing:
- Summarize in writing, with specific next steps and dates.

## Language that works (mostly moderate-strong)
- **Concrete over abstract**: "save 4 hours a week" beats "boost efficiency." Concrete words are easier to process and remember.
- **Specific numbers** feel more credible than round ones (moderate).
- **Plain, short sentences** reduce load; processing fluency increases perceived truth and liking (strong).
- **"You" and the reader's goals** increase relevance.
- **Stories** (setup, problem, struggle, turning point, outcome) transport people and reduce counter-arguing (moderate). Let the lesson emerge rather than lecturing.
- **Rhythm**: groups of three, contrasts ("not X, but Y"), and repetition make lines memorable.
- **Presupposition** ("When you start...") and embedded commands are pressure techniques; mild versions are common, but they cross into manipulation when they hide a choice. Prefer honest framing.
- **Cut**: filler words, hedges stacked on hedges, jargon, and any sentence that adds nothing.

## Resistance and counter-persuasion
- **Reactance** (Brehm): pressure on freedom triggers pushback. Reduce it by offering real choices, acknowledging "it's your call", shrinking the ask, and using questions instead of commands.
- **Motivational Interviewing** (Miller & Rollnick; strong in health settings): open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries. Draw out the person's own reasons for change instead of arguing for it; arguing (the "righting reflex") provokes defense of the status quo.
- **Inoculation / prebunking** (McGuire; van der Linden; strong): exposing people to weakened manipulation techniques plus refutation builds resistance.
- **Two-sided messages** that address counterarguments work better for skeptical or informed audiences.
- **Debunking**: replace a false claim with a correct explanation, not just "that's wrong" (continued influence effect). The "backfire effect" (corrections strengthening false beliefs) is rare.
- **Forewarning** that someone will try to persuade reduces persuasion but increases careful thinking.
- **Focus on the key belief**: change one load-bearing belief instead of arguing with everything.
