# Motivation: Why People Start and Keep Going

## Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan) — strong evidence
Three basic needs predict lasting, high-quality motivation and wellbeing across cultures:
- **Autonomy**: feeling that you chose the activity and it reflects your values. Supported by meaningful choices, explanations of "why", and lack of pressure.
- **Competence**: feeling effective and improving. Supported by optimal challenge, clear feedback, visible progress.
- **Relatedness**: feeling connected and cared about. Supported by teams, community, shared goals.

Motivation runs on a spectrum: amotivation -> external (rewards/punishment) -> introjected (guilt, ego) -> identified (I value it) -> integrated -> intrinsic (enjoyable in itself). The more internal types predict persistence, creativity, and wellbeing.

## Rewards and the overjustification effect
- Expected, tangible rewards made contingent on doing an already-enjoyable task can reduce intrinsic motivation once rewards stop (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 meta-analysis; moderate-strong, debated in size).
- Rewards that are unexpected, informational ("you mastered this"), or for boring tasks do much less harm.
- Verbal praise for competence tends to increase intrinsic motivation; controlling praise ("you should...") reduces it.

## Expectancy x Value
People pursue a goal when they expect they can succeed AND value the result (Eccles, Vroom). Value includes enjoyment, usefulness, identity fit, and cost (effort, lost alternatives). Raise either term; if one is zero, motivation is zero.

## Self-efficacy (Bandura) — strong
Belief in your ability to do a specific task predicts effort and persistence. Built most by **mastery experiences** (small wins), then by watching similar others succeed, encouragement, and managing anxiety.

## Goals
- **Goal-setting theory** (Locke & Latham, strong): specific, challenging goals with feedback beat "do your best", provided people have the skills and commitment. For complex new tasks, learning goals beat performance goals.
- **Goal-gradient effect** (Hull; Kivetz et al. 2006, moderate-strong): effort increases as people near a goal. Showing progress (even "artificial" head starts, like a loyalty card pre-stamped 2/12) speeds completion.
- **Implementation intentions** ("When X happens, I will do Y") roughly double follow-through in many studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis; moderate-strong, smaller in recent preregistered work).
- **Mental contrasting (WOOP)**: imagining the goal plus the obstacle beats positive fantasy alone, which can reduce effort (moderate).

## Flow (Csikszentmihalyi)
Deep absorption happens when challenge roughly matches skill, goals are clear, and feedback is immediate. Too hard -> anxiety; too easy -> boredom. This is the core of good game and learning design.

## Curiosity and the information gap
Curiosity peaks when people know a little but not enough (Loewenstein's information-gap theory). Mysteries, cliffhangers, locked areas, and "?" icons exploit this. Curiosity also activates reward circuitry and improves memory for nearby information (Gruber et al. 2014).

## Procrastination — why people don't start
Temporal Motivation Theory (Steel 2007): Motivation = (Expectancy x Value) / (Impulsiveness x Delay). People delay when the task feels unlikely to succeed, unpleasant or meaningless, far off, or when they are easily distracted. Procrastination is mostly **mood repair**: avoiding the bad feeling a task triggers (Sirois & Pychyl). Fixes: shrink the first step, add immediate rewards, reduce ambiguity, set near deadlines, remove distractions.

## Maslow's hierarchy — use with caution
The idea that basic needs matter and people seek meaning is reasonable, but the strict pyramid order is not supported. Needs operate together.

## Other motivators worth knowing
- **Status and social comparison**: people care about relative standing; leaderboards motivate those near the top and can demotivate those at the bottom.
- **Identity**: "I am a runner" sustains behavior better than "I'm trying to run".
- **Meaning/purpose**: seeing how work helps others increases effort (Grant's fundraiser studies; moderate).
- **Fear and threat**: can trigger action if paired with a clear, doable response; otherwise causes avoidance (Extended Parallel Process Model).
