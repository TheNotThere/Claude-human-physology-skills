# Memory and Learning

## Systems
- **Working memory** (Baddeley): phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer, central executive. Capacity about 4 chunks (Cowan 2001), not the old "7 plus or minus 2".
- **Long-term memory**: declarative (episodic events, semantic facts; hippocampus and neocortex) vs nondeclarative (skills, conditioning, priming).
- **Consolidation**: the hippocampus replays experiences, especially during sleep, gradually strengthening cortical storage.
- **Retrieval is reconstruction**, not playback. Memories shift with each recall and can be distorted by suggestion (Loftus misinformation effect; strong).

## Encoding principles (mostly strong)
- **Depth of processing** (Craik & Lockhart): thinking about meaning beats repetition.
- **Self-reference effect**: information linked to yourself is remembered better.
- **Generation effect**: producing an answer beats reading it.
- **Encoding specificity**: recall is better when retrieval context matches learning context (moderate, smaller in real life).
- **Emotional events** are remembered more vividly, though not always more accurately.

## Desirable difficulties (Bjork) — strong
- **Spacing**: spread practice out. A workable review schedule: 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2-3 weeks, 1 month.
- **Retrieval practice / testing effect** (Roediger & Karpicke 2006): testing yourself beats rereading on delayed tests.
- **Interleaving**: mixing problem types helps learners choose the right method (strong in math and category learning).
- These feel harder and less fluent, so learners avoid them. Fluency (ease of reading) is often mistaken for knowledge.

## Metacognition
- People are often poorly calibrated about what they know. Predicting your score before a test and comparing it to the result improves calibration.
- **Dunning-Kruger**: the popular "incompetent people think they're experts" version overstates it; part of the pattern is a statistical artifact. Low performers do overestimate somewhat.

## Familiarity vs recall
- **Mere exposure** (Zajonc): repeated exposure increases liking and feelings of familiarity without producing recall.
- **Illusory truth effect**: repeated statements feel more true, even known falsehoods (strong). A reason misinformation spreads and a reason to repeat correct information.

## Applying it
- For learning: retrieve, space, interleave, explain in your own words ("this means that... because..."), and connect to personal goals.
- For games and apps: teach one mechanic at a time, have players use it right away, then bring it back later in a new situation.
- For messages: one clear idea, repeated across exposures, tied to the audience's own situation.

## Myths
- Learning styles matching: not supported.
- "We forget 90% in a week" figures: depends heavily on material and measurement.
- Photographic memory in adults: essentially no reliable evidence.
