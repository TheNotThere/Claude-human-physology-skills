# Persuasion, Influence, and Power

## How persuasion works
- **Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty & Cacioppo): when people are motivated and able to think, argument quality matters most (central route, longer-lasting change). When distracted or uninvolved, cues like attractiveness, expertise, and number of arguments dominate (peripheral route, weaker change).
- **Reactance** (Brehm): pressure that threatens freedom makes people resist and do the opposite. Phrases like "but you are free to choose" increase compliance (meta-analysis by Carpenter 2013; moderate).
- **Inoculation**: warning people about manipulation and showing weakened examples builds resistance (strong).
- People are persuaded more by those they see as similar, likeable, and credible, and by messages that fit their identity and values (moral reframing).

## Cialdini's principles of influence
1. **Reciprocity**: people feel obliged to return favors. Free samples and genuine help work. (Robust.)
2. **Commitment and consistency**: small agreements lead to larger ones (foot-in-the-door; real but small effects in meta-analyses). Public, written, voluntary commitments stick best.
3. **Social proof**: people follow similar others, especially under uncertainty. Descriptive norms ("most guests reuse towels") work, though some specific famous effects are smaller than first reported.
4. **Authority**: expertise and credentials increase compliance (Milgram's obedience studies are informative but debated in interpretation).
5. **Liking**: similarity, compliments, cooperation, and familiarity increase influence.
6. **Scarcity**: limited availability increases desirability, especially when newly scarce or competed for. Faking it is deceptive.
7. **Unity**: shared identity ("we", family, team) is a strong basis for influence.

## Other influence findings
- **Door-in-the-face**: a large request then a smaller one boosts compliance with the smaller one (moderate).
- **"Because" effect**: giving a reason increases compliance (the classic copier study is small; giving real reasons is still good practice).
- **Mere exposure**: repeated exposure increases liking, up to a point (moderate-strong).
- **Storytelling**: narratives reduce counter-arguing and increase persuasion (moderate).
- **Two-sided messages** that address counterarguments work better with informed or skeptical audiences.

## The 48 Laws of Power (Robert Greene, 1998)
A popular book of strategic advice drawn from historical anecdotes (courts, generals, con artists). It is not research, and its examples are selected to fit its points. Its main themes, summarized:
- **Reputation and image**: guard your reputation, control how others see you, court attention, appear confident.
- **Concealment and unpredictability**: conceal intentions, say less than necessary, be unpredictable, keep others dependent.
- **Using others**: let others do the work and take credit, use people's self-interest, find their weaknesses.
- **Strategic positioning**: never outshine your superiors, avoid unlucky or unhappy people, choose battles, strike decisively.
- **Timing and adaptability**: be patient, adapt your form, know when to stop.

How it holds up against psychology:
- Some ideas have support in research on status and influence: confidence and competence signals do raise perceived status (Anderson & Kilduff), reputation matters, and people do respond to self-interest.
- Many tactics are manipulative and short-term. Research on organizations finds that trust, fairness, and generosity tend to produce more durable influence (Adam Grant's "givers"; the "power paradox" research by Keltner finding people gain power through empathy and collaboration but lose it when they act selfishly). Machiavellian tactics predict short-term gains but more conflict, turnover, and lost trust.
- Use the book to recognize manipulation and understand political environments, not as a playbook for treating people.

## When Claude helps with persuasion
Help users make honest cases persuasively, understand others' perspectives, negotiate fairly, and recognize manipulation. Do not help craft deception, exploit someone's fear or vulnerability, pressure people against their interests, or isolate or control a specific person.
