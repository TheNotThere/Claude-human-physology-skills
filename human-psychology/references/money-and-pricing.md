# Money, Pricing, and Buying Psychology

## How people think about money
- **Mental accounting** (Thaler): people label money into categories (rent, fun, windfall) and treat it differently; windfalls get spent more freely.
- **Pain of paying**: cash feels more painful than cards; subscriptions and stored balances reduce payment pain (moderate). Virtual currencies in games increase spending by obscuring real prices.
- **Present bias**: buy now, pay later and free trials exploit preference for immediate benefits.
- **Reference prices**: prices are judged against expectations, past prices, and nearby options.

## Pricing effects
- **Anchoring**: showing a higher "original" price raises perceived value (robust; fake anchors are illegal in many places).
- **Charm pricing** ($9.99): left-digit bias makes prices feel lower (moderate).
- **Decoy effect**: a dominated third option can push choices toward a target (mixed replication; works in some markets).
- **Price as quality signal**: higher prices can increase expected quality and even experienced quality for some goods (moderate).
- **Bundling** reduces attention to individual prices; **partitioned pricing** (separate fees) can make totals seem lower; drip pricing is deceptive and increasingly regulated.
- **Free** has outsized pull compared with very low prices (zero-price effect; moderate).
- **Subscription traps**: easy signup, hard cancellation; regulators (FTC, EU) target these.

## Why people buy
Solving a problem, identity and status, social proof, emotional reward, curiosity, convenience, reducing risk (guarantees, returns), and trust in the seller.

## Why people don't buy
Uncertain value, too many choices, risk of regret, effort, unclear price, distrust, wrong timing, or the status quo feels fine.

## Ethical pricing checklist
Real reference prices; all fees shown upfront; real deadlines; easy cancellation; clear odds and real-money equivalents for in-game currency; spending limits and no gambling-like paid randomness for minors.
