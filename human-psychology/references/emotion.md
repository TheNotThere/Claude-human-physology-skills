# Emotion

## Structure
- **Core affect** (Russell): any feeling can be placed on two dimensions, pleasant-unpleasant (valence) and calm-activated (arousal).
- **Appraisal theories** (Lazarus; Scherer): emotions come from how a person evaluates an event: Is it relevant? Good or bad for my goals? Can I cope? Is it fair? The same event triggers different emotions depending on appraisal.
- **Debate**: basic emotion theory (Ekman: a few universal emotions with recognizable faces) vs constructed emotion (Barrett: the brain builds emotions from body signals, concepts, and context). Evidence supports some cross-cultural recognition but much more variability and context-dependence than classic claims. Present both.

## Mood and thinking (moderate)
- Positive mood tends to broaden attention and increase creative, heuristic thinking (Isen; Fredrickson).
- Mild negative mood tends to increase careful, detail-focused processing (Forgas; Schwarz).
- **Affect as information**: people use their current feelings as evidence when judging unrelated things ("How's my life?" on a sunny day). The classic weather study's specific effect replicated poorly, but the general idea has support.
- **Facial feedback**: small at best; the 2016 multi-lab replication of the pen-in-mouth study found no effect, while later work found small effects under some conditions.

## Emotion regulation (Gross's process model; strong)
Strategies, from earlier to later in the emotion: choose or change the situation, shift attention, reappraise (reinterpret the meaning), suppress the expression. Reappraisal generally works better and costs less than suppression, which tends to increase physiological stress and reduce rapport. Naming an emotion ("affect labeling") can modestly reduce its intensity (Lieberman).

## Emotions that drive behavior
- **Fear** motivates action only when people believe a clear response will work (self-efficacy). Otherwise it causes avoidance and denial.
- **Anger** increases risk-taking and certainty and reduces careful thinking.
- **Guilt** (I did a bad thing) motivates repair; **shame** (I am bad) motivates hiding and defensiveness.
- **Awe** reduces focus on self and increases generosity in some studies (moderate).
- **Boredom** pushes people to seek novelty, a key driver of quitting a game or task.
- **Anticipated regret** often matters more than the emotion felt in the moment.
- **Frustration** is the main short-term quitting emotion in games; **relief after tension** is one of the strongest positive moments.

## Applying it
- Match tone to the audience's state: high-stakes, detail-hungry audiences want calm, clear evidence; low-involvement audiences respond more to feeling and story.
- Design emotional arcs: tension then release, challenge then mastery, and end on a high (peak-end rule).
- Don't manufacture fear or shame to drive action; it backfires on trust and often on behavior.
