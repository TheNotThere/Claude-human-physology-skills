# The Brain, Reward, and Learning

## Dopamine: wanting, not liking
- Dopamine neurons in the midbrain (VTA, substantia nigra) project to the striatum and prefrontal cortex. Their best-supported role is the **reward prediction error**: they fire more when a reward is better than expected, stay flat when it is exactly as expected, and dip when it is worse (Schultz; strong evidence).
- Consequence: **surprise and uncertainty drive learning and attention.** Fully predictable rewards stop exciting the system. This is why random rewards (loot drops, social media likes) are so gripping.
- Berridge's **wanting vs liking** distinction: dopamine drives wanting (craving, pursuit, cue-triggered urges). Liking (actual pleasure) relies more on opioid and endocannabinoid "hotspots". People can want something intensely that they no longer enjoy. This explains compulsive scrolling or playing that feels empty (strong in animals, good support in humans).
- Cues become rewarding: a sound, icon, or notification paired with reward starts triggering wanting by itself (incentive salience).

## Two broad modes of control
- **Goal-directed** behavior (prefrontal cortex, dorsomedial striatum) is flexible and sensitive to outcomes.
- **Habitual** behavior (dorsolateral striatum) is automatic, triggered by cues, and continues even when the reward is gone.
- Repetition in a stable context shifts control from goal-directed to habit. Stress pushes people toward habits.
- "System 1 vs System 2" (fast intuitive vs slow deliberate) is a useful metaphor, not two literal brain systems. Treat it as a description, not anatomy.

## Emotion and decision-making
- The amygdala flags threat and relevance. The ventromedial prefrontal cortex integrates emotion into valuation; damage there impairs real-life decisions (Damasio). Emotion is part of good judgment, not its enemy.
- Anticipated emotions (expected regret, pride, fear) strongly guide choices.

## Stress
- Short, manageable stress can sharpen focus. Chronic or overwhelming stress impairs prefrontal function (planning, self-control, flexible thinking) and increases habitual and impulsive behavior.
- Practical implication: people under stress fall back on defaults and habits, so design for the stressed version of the user.

## Attention and working memory
- Working memory holds roughly 4 chunks at once (Cowan). Overload causes errors, avoidance, and abandoning tasks.
- Attention is captured by novelty, motion, faces, one's own name, and reward-associated cues.
- Multitasking is mostly fast switching, with a cost each switch.

## Memory and experience
- **Peak-end rule**: people judge past experiences largely by the most intense moment and the ending, not the average or duration (Kahneman; moderate-strong). End sessions, levels, and meetings on a high note.
- **Spacing and retrieval practice**: spreading practice out and testing yourself beats rereading (strong; Dunlosky et al. 2013).
- Memories are reconstructed each time they are recalled and can change.

## Sleep, energy, and self-control
- Sleep loss reliably impairs attention, mood, and self-control (strong).
- The "willpower is a battery" idea (ego depletion) largely failed large multi-lab replications. Better framing: motivation and attention shift with fatigue, boredom, and how much people value the task. Low blood sugar "recharging" willpower did not replicate.

## Common brain myths to correct
- Left-brain/right-brain personality types: false.
- We use only 10% of our brain: false.
- "Lizard brain" vs "human brain" layers (triune brain): outdated.
- Dopamine = pleasure: oversimplified (see above).
- Learning styles (visual/auditory learners need matched teaching): not supported.
