# Game Psychology: Why People Play, Stay, and Quit

## Why people play (player motivations)
- **SDT in games** (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006, PENS): enjoyment and continued play are predicted by competence (mastery, satisfying challenge), autonomy (meaningful choices, freedom), and relatedness (playing with others). Strong and widely replicated.
- **Yee's online gaming motivations**: Achievement (advancement, mechanics, competition), Social (socializing, relationships, teamwork), Immersion (discovery, role-playing, customization, escapism).
- **Quantic Foundry model** (survey data from many players): Action (destruction, excitement), Social (competition, community), Mastery (challenge, strategy), Achievement (completion, power), Immersion (fantasy, story), Creativity (design, discovery). Players differ, so know your audience.
- **Bartle's player types** (Achievers, Explorers, Socializers, Killers): a useful historical shorthand from MUDs, not a validated typology.
- Other drivers: escapism and stress relief, fantasy and identity play, curiosity, self-expression, collection, and status.

## Key factors that make a game appealing
1. **Tight core loop**: a short action -> feedback -> reward -> new goal cycle that feels good in seconds (shoot, loot, upgrade; match, clear, score).
2. **Juice / game feel**: responsive controls, sound, animation, screen shake, particle effects. Strong feedback makes simple actions satisfying.
3. **Flow-tuned difficulty**: challenge rises with skill; alternating tension and relief (waves, boss then calm). Dynamic difficulty helps when done invisibly and fairly.
4. **Clear goals at three scales**: immediate (this fight), session (this level), long-term (story, rank, collection).
5. **Visible progression**: XP, levels, skill trees, unlocks, maps filling in. Goal-gradient and endowed progress apply.
6. **Meaningful choice**: builds, routes, playstyles, story decisions that change outcomes (autonomy).
7. **Mastery depth**: easy to learn, hard to master. Hidden depth keeps skilled players engaged.
8. **Variable rewards and surprise**: random drops, rare events, secrets. Powerful via reward prediction error.
9. **Curiosity hooks**: fog of war, locked doors, mysteries, cliffhangers (information-gap theory).
10. **Social play**: co-op, guilds, trading, friendly competition, shared events. Relatedness and social commitment drive retention.
11. **Story and character attachment**: narrative transportation increases emotional investment.
12. **Collection and completion**: sets, achievements, 100% completion.
13. **Self-expression**: customization, building, creating content.
14. **Low-friction onboarding**: teach by doing, early wins in the first minutes, no walls of text.
15. **Fair failure**: quick restarts, clear cause of death, learning from mistakes. Players blame unfair games and quit.

## Retention mechanics (and their honest effects)
- **Daily rewards, streaks, quests**: build habits through fixed-interval schedules. Effective, but can make play feel like an obligation ("chore-ification").
- **Appointment mechanics / energy timers**: bring players back at intervals; often frustrating and paired with paid skips.
- **Seasonal content and battle passes**: novelty plus goal-gradient plus fear of missing out.
- **Social obligation**: guild raids, team commitments. Strong retention; also strong pressure.
- **Sunk cost**: long-time players stay because of invested time. Not a reason they enjoy it.

The healthiest retention comes from players **wanting** to return (fun, mastery, friends, new content), not feeling unable to stop.

## Why players quit
Frustration spikes, grinding without progress, unfair competition or pay-to-win, toxic communities, no new goals at endgame, poor onboarding, feeling manipulated by monetization, and life changes.

## Monetization psychology
- Purchases are driven by convenience, cosmetics and status, power, and gambling-like randomness.
- **Loot boxes**: meta-analyses find small-to-moderate correlations between loot box spending and problem gambling symptoms (around r = .26-.27; Spicer et al. 2021; Garea et al.). A small share of buyers produces a large share of revenue, and many of them score as problem gamblers. Causality is unclear. Several countries regulate them.
- Ethical alternatives: direct-purchase cosmetics with clear prices, published odds, spending limits, no randomized paid items for minors, no hidden currency conversions.

## Gaming disorder
Recognized by the WHO (ICD-11). Affects a small minority of players. Most gaming is healthy and can benefit mood, social connection, and some cognitive skills. Signs of a problem: loss of control, priority over other life areas, continuing despite harm, for 12+ months.
