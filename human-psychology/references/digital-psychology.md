# Digital Psychology: Apps, Social Media, Attention

## The attention economy
Many products are optimized for time-on-app. The main levers: infinite feeds (no stopping cues), variable social rewards (likes, comments, new content), notifications (external triggers), autoplay, social comparison, streaks, and personalization that learns what grabs each person.

## Why feeds are hard to stop
- Variable ratio reinforcement and reward prediction error: the next post might be great.
- No natural stopping points; studies with "bottomless" soup bowls show people consume more without visual cues to stop (Wansink's specific work was later discredited for misconduct, so cite stopping-cue research generally rather than that study).
- Short videos deliver frequent novelty with low effort.
- Fear of missing out and social comparison.

## Social media and mental health: the evidence (contested)
- Large meta-analyses mostly find small average associations between social media use and lower wellbeing or depressive symptoms; experiments reducing use show small benefits at best. Haidt and colleagues argue the effects are meaningful, especially for teen girls and heavy users, and point to timing of population trends and internal company documents; Odgers, Orben, Ferguson and others argue effects are too small or unclear to be "the major cause" of youth mental health trends.
- Points most researchers accept: average effects are small; heavy and problematic use, sleep displacement, cyberbullying, and certain content (self-harm, eating disorder content) carry more risk; individual differences are large.
- Present both sides, and note that "small average effect" can coexist with real harm for a subset.

## Notifications and interruptions
Interruptions cost time to refocus and increase errors and stress; batching notifications reduces stress (moderate). Phone presence alone reducing cognitive capacity ("brain drain" study) did not replicate well.

## Designing for wellbeing (humane technology)
- Add stopping cues (end of feed, session summaries, time reminders users choose).
- Default notifications to fewer and grouped.
- Make leaving and pausing easy; no guilt messaging.
- Let users set goals for their own use.
- Measure success by user-endorsed value (satisfaction, goals met), not only time spent.
- Friction for unwanted habits: pause screens before opening an app and greyscale modes help some users (small-moderate effects).

## Blocking and self-control tools
They work by adding friction and pre-commitment (deciding in advance when calm). Pre-commitment is strongly supported in behavioral economics. People often relapse via workarounds, so combine with replacing the habit and changing context.
