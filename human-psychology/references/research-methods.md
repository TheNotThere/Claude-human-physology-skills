# How to Judge Psychology Research

Use this whenever evaluating a finding, a study the user shares, or a claim from a book or article.

## Red flags
- Small samples (dozens per group) with surprising, large effects.
- Single study, no replication; results only from one lab.
- p-values just under .05, many measures with one reported, flexible analyses ("p-hacking", "garden of forking paths").
- No preregistration; outcomes or hypotheses changed after seeing data.
- Lab tasks claimed to explain big life outcomes.
- Correlation presented as causation.
- WEIRD-only samples generalized to everyone.
- Press release or book claims stronger than the paper.
- Author has commercial interest in the result.

## Green flags
Preregistration, registered reports, large samples, multi-lab replications, meta-analyses that correct for publication bias, open data and code, effects shown in real-world settings, a clear mechanism, and consistent results across cultures and methods.

## Effect sizes in plain language
- Cohen's d around 0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large (rough conventions). Correlations r of 0.1 = small, 0.3 = medium, 0.5 = large.
- Many real psychological effects are small (d 0.1-0.3). Small effects can still matter at scale, but they rarely transform individuals.
- Relative vs absolute: "doubles the risk" may mean 1% to 2%.

## Study types (weaker to stronger for causal claims)
Anecdote -> cross-sectional correlation -> longitudinal correlation -> natural experiment -> randomized experiment -> replicated randomized experiments -> preregistered multi-lab replication / bias-corrected meta-analysis.

## Meta-analysis caveats
Garbage in, garbage out; publication bias inflates pooled effects; heterogeneity means the average may describe no one. Check whether bias-correction was done and whether effects vary by setting.

## How to phrase claims
"Strong evidence shows", "studies suggest", "early or mixed evidence", "this famous finding failed to replicate". Give the size and the setting when possible.
