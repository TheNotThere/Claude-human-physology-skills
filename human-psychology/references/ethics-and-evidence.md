# Ethics and Evidence

## The engagement vs exploitation test
Before advising on how to change other people's behavior, check:
1. **Transparency**: would it still work if the person knew exactly how it worked? If revealing it would make them angry, it is manipulation.
2. **Endorsement**: would they thank you later? Does it serve their goals, not only yours?
3. **Freedom**: can they easily say no, leave, cancel, or undo?
4. **Vulnerability**: does it target children, people with addictions, people in crisis, lonely people, or people in debt?
5. **Truth**: is every claim, number, deadline, and review real?

If a design fails these, say so, explain the risk (legal, reputational, user trust), and propose an ethical alternative.

## Dark patterns to recognize and avoid
- Fake scarcity or countdown timers; fake social proof ("23 people viewing").
- Confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't like saving money").
- Roach motel: easy to sign up, hard to cancel.
- Hidden costs, drip pricing, pre-checked add-ons.
- Disguised ads, bait-and-switch, nagging.
- Obscured virtual currency prices; paid randomized rewards with hidden odds.
- Infinite scroll and autoplay with no natural stopping points (stopping cues help users regain control).
- Exploiting fear of losing streaks or social status to drive spending.
Many of these are now regulated (for example, EU Digital Services Act and consumer law, US FTC actions on subscription traps and dark patterns, several countries' loot box rules).

## Special care groups
- **Children and teens**: developing impulse control and high sensitivity to social reward. No gambling-like mechanics, no manipulative spending, no social pressure tactics.
- **People with addictions or problem gambling**: variable money rewards and near misses can do real harm.
- **People in emotional distress**: avoid pressure, urgency, or exploiting loneliness.

## Evidence strength guide (as of 2026)
Strong / well-replicated:
- Reinforcement schedules; reward prediction error; spacing and retrieval practice; self-efficacy; goal-setting; SDT needs; defaults; anchoring (classic paradigms); framing; present bias; peak-end rule; mere exposure; inoculation.
Moderate / context-dependent:
- Loss aversion (real on average, smaller or absent for small stakes); implementation intentions; foot-in-the-door; overjustification; goal-gradient; social-norm messages; choice overload (only under certain conditions); nudges in general (small average effects after bias correction).
Weak or failed replication:
- Ego depletion (null in multi-lab replications of 2,141 and 3,531 participants); glucose restoring willpower; power posing hormone effects; behavioral priming (e.g., walking slowly after elderly words); money priming; the marshmallow test as a strong life predictor; facial feedback (mixed); Zeigarnik memory effect; decision fatigue in judges; 21-day habits; learning styles; Stanford Prison Experiment as evidence of natural cruelty.

More claims to hedge or correct:
- Loss framing beats gain framing "by 1.5-2x": false; meta-analyses (O'Keefe & Jensen) find little overall difference between gain and loss frames.
- "Every extra step loses an order of magnitude of conversions": invented precision. Friction matters; the size varies widely.
- Warm drinks make people warmer toward others: failed replication.
- Oxytocin as a general trust hormone: weak.
- Strong polyvagal claims; Dunning-Kruger as popularly told; power posing hormones; strong choice overload; facial feedback as a large effect: hedge all.
- Profiling strangers by attachment style or personality to target them: unreliable and ethically risky.
- Lowball (agree, then raise the price) and fake urgency: deceptive; don't recommend.
- Ideal email send times and similar marketing folklore: test it rather than assume.

Rule: when citing a finding, say how solid it is. When unsure, say "research suggests" and note limits.
